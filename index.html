Why contact-rich assembly is still unsolved

Assembly is the largest remaining manual step in modern manufacturing, and insertion — mating a part to a receptacle under sub-millimetre tolerance — is its atomic operation. Automating it reliably is the difference between a demonstration and a production line.

Yet a decade of deep reinforcement learning, and the current wave of vision–language–action models, have not produced insertion policies that survive contact with a real production line. Reported successes cluster on simple pegs and generous clearances, lean on thousands of GPU-hours of simulation, and fall apart on the electronic connectors and fasteners that factories actually assemble.

The reason is that what governs success is barely observable to a camera: friction, jamming, clamping, deformation, spring-loaded force barriers. This information never enters the observation stream, so no amount of data or model capacity recovers it. Our claim is narrow and concrete — the missing ingredient is not scale but model knowledge: the physical structure of the robot, its contact interaction, and the task process, encoded in the policy itself. Supply that structure and insertion becomes reliable; withhold it and learning breaks — and so does the hardware.

What model knowledge is made of

Model knowledge is not one thing. It resolves into three layers of physical structure — the robot's own embodiment, its interaction with the environment, and the process of the task itself. Each layer can be supplied explicitly or acquired by learning, but none can be absent from a representation capable of reliable contact-rich manipulation. The search or learning mechanism is listed separately: it is not itself model knowledge.

A benchmark for probing model knowledge in learning

The 22 objects are not chosen for visual variety but for the physical structure they demand of a policy. Each is a real electronic connector, fastener or mechanism — plugs, sockets, keys, locks — spanning the effects that make insertion hard: tight clearances, interference fits, clamping, deformation, friction and spring-loaded force barriers. Because these effects are invisible to a camera and only revealed on contact, the set isolates exactly the knowledge a policy must carry rather than perceive. Running the full model-knowledge hierarchy against every object turns the benchmark into a controlled probe: it shows which structural ingredient each task needs, and where a policy that lacks it fails. Success basins are narrow, multimodal, and hard to transfer

The parameters that make an insertion succeed do not form a single broad region. Mapping the feasible policy space reveals narrow, often disconnected basins — several distinct settings can solve the same task, while tiny departures from any of them fail. Because each basin is shaped by the specific contact physics of its object, the solution for one task rarely transfers to another: what works for a snug socket is the wrong strategy for a chamfered pin or a spring-loaded barrier. This is why a single learned policy does not generalise across the benchmark, and why the structure a policy carries matters more than the volume of data it sees.

When structure is missing, hardware breaks

The cost of missing model knowledge is not only failed insertions — it is destroyed components. Without the correct physical structure the policy applies force without the right precision or direction, and failed attempts damage the parts. Destruction rises sharply with action scaling; the force domain at Level IV alone damaged 26–31 objects. The structured Tactile Skill, by contrast, produces no damage beyond normal operational wear — its impedance structure implicitly bounds contact forces regardless of the learned parameters.

Learning the industrial workflows

Each industrial workflow is a chain of contact-rich subtasks — grasping, mating, seating, fastening — and every one is acquired as a structured Tactile Skill directly on the real line, without simulation. Learning is fast: every subtask across all four partner assemblies is learned within 30 minutes, after which the system runs the full workflows autonomously. Four industrial workflows, fully autonomous

Four complete assembly workflows were automated end to end and run without human intervention: a bottle-gripper assembly, a control-cabinet build, a bus-module insertion, and a coupling test. Each chains several contact-rich subtasks and executes at industrial cycle times. Throughput is reported relative to a skilled human operator performing the same workflow; success is the fraction of fully completed cycles.

Limitations

  • The Tactile Skill assumes a known goal pose. In this study the target pose is provided; in practice it can be obtained from a single visual teach-in or a standard vision system, but the method does not itself solve target localisation.
  • The approach requires an embodiment model — robot dynamics, the underlying control principles (impedance control, feedforward force), and in many cases a model of the manipulated environment. It presumes a torque-controlled manipulator with reliable external-torque estimation, which among commercial arms is dependably available on the Franka Emika robot.
  • The claim is deliberately narrow: it concerns contact-rich tasks governed by sub-millimetre clearances and physical effects (pressure, deformation, interference fit, clamping, friction) whose control information is absent from the visual observation stream. It is not a claim about the limits of learning in general, and does not dispute the progress of large behaviour or vision–language–action models on visually rich tasks.
  • The benchmark, while spanning diverse geometries and interaction regimes, does not exhaust the full range of robotic embodiments, materials, or assembly conditions found in industrial practice.
  • Industrial throughput and cost figures (150–200% of human daily throughput as a dual-arm system; up to 400% of human productivity per unit labour cost) hold under the evaluated deployment conditions and should not be read as guarantees across arbitrary lines.
